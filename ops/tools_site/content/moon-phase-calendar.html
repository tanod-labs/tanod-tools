How the calendar is calculated

For each day the page computes the Moon's and Sun's ecliptic longitudes and distances at 12:00 in the chosen time zone, using the series in Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms (2nd ed., 1998): the Sun from chapter 25 and the Moon from chapter 47. The elongation is the Moon's longitude minus the Sun's, 0° at new moon, 90° at first quarter, 180° at full moon and 270° at last quarter. The phase name comes from the elongation in 45° steps.

The illuminated fraction is k = (1 + cos i) / 2, where i is the phase angle found from the Moon-Sun-Earth triangle (Meeus, chapter 48, formula 48.3). The exact times of the principal phases are found by scanning the elongation every six hours and bisecting each crossing of 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° to about a second. The synodic month used for reference is 29.530588853 days.

The same engine is behind the sun and moon API, which is tested against the JPL DE421 ephemeris: illumination is within 0.5 percentage points. The result is an astronomical calculation, not for navigation, and the visible shape also depends on libration and your latitude.
