What happens when a web page speaks

Text to speech in a browser is a hand-off. The page gives your text, a voice name, a speed and a pitch to the browser, and the browser asks the operating system, or in some cases the browser maker's servers, to turn it into sound. That is why two people on the same page hear different voices, and why there is nothing here to download: the page itself never receives audio.

On-device voices are the ones your system ships with, such as those installed with Windows, macOS, iOS or Android, and they work offline. Network voices usually sound more natural, which is why browsers offer them, but the text has to travel to a server to be spoken. That difference matters for private material, so check the (online) mark before you paste anything sensitive.

Listening is a good proofreading trick. Tired eyes slide over a repeated word or a missing full stop, but a voice reads exactly what is written and stumbles where the sentence does. Slow the speed a little and follow the highlighted word.

Tips

  • Check how long a passage will take to read first with the word counter, which gives a speaking-time estimate.
  • Strip stray line breaks from copied text with remove line breaks so sentences are not read in fragments.
  • Make sure the right symbols and capitals are in place using the case converter before listening.
