      {{mark:i-alert}}An estimate, not a guarantee. Percentage used is the drive's own estimate and can pass 100%. A healthy reading does not rule out sudden failure, so keep backups of anything you cannot replace.

      NVMe percentage used: what it means

      Percentage used is a field in the NVMe SMART log. The drive's controller estimates how much of the flash's rated endurance has been consumed, from how much has really been written and the maker's model of how many write cycles the cells survive. A new drive reads 0%. At 100% the estimated endurance is used up, but the specification says that does not by itself mean the drive has failed, and the value is allowed to keep rising past 100%. Because it is the drive's own estimate, two models can differ for the same amount of data.

      It is not the same as how full the drive is, and it is not remaining life in years. A drive used for light office work can sit at 3% for years, while a drive used for video capture or databases can reach 100% in months. What matters is the trend, so check again in a month and compare.

      Data units written and TBW

      The same log counts Data Units Written. One unit is 1,000 blocks of 512 bytes, so 512,000 bytes. Multiply the count by 512,000 for the bytes the host has written, and divide by 10^12 for decimal terabytes. Makers publish an endurance rating in TBW (terabytes written) for the warranty, usually on the spec sheet for the exact model and capacity. Comparing the two figures is a cross-check: if the written share of the rating is far below percentage used, the drive is wearing faster than its rating suggests; if it is far above, the rating was conservative.

      How to check a used SSD before you buy it

      1. Ask the seller for the full smartctl -a or nvme smart-log output, not a screenshot of one tool's summary score.
      2. Look at percentage used first. Under about 20% is lightly worn; above 80% is near the end of its design life; at or over 100% is past it.
      3. Check available spare against its threshold. Spare falling toward the threshold means the drive is running out of replacement blocks.
      4. Check media and data integrity errors and the critical warning flags. Non-zero errors, or any warning flag other than temperature, are reasons to walk away.
      5. Read power-on hours and data written together. A drive with a few hours and many terabytes, or the other way round, deserves questions, because counters can be reset.
      6. Unsafe shutdowns are a clue to how the drive was treated, not a failure by themselves.
      7. Run a full read test (for example smartctl -t long) and re-read the log afterwards to see whether any counter moved.

      What SMART's PASSED line misses

      The overall self-assessment only flips to FAILED when the drive crosses its own failure limits, which can be very late. A drive can pass with 95% used and a handful of media errors. The numbers above show wear and early trouble well before the headline result changes.
