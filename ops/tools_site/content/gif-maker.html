    How an animated GIF is put together

    A GIF is a stack of ordinary pictures shown one after another. Each frame carries its own small colour table of at most 256 colours and a delay, a time in hundredths of a second before the next frame appears. A loop setting at the start tells the viewer whether to repeat forever or stop after one pass. There is no video compression: every frame is stored whole, packed with a simple lossless method, which is why GIFs get big quickly.

    Making one from photos means choosing the 256 colours that best stand for each picture and then replacing every pixel with the nearest of them. This tool does that for each frame separately with the open-source gifenc encoder, after drawing every picture at the same size so the frames line up. The size comes from the width you choose and the proportions of the first picture, and the other pictures are fitted inside it, cropped to fill it or stretched, whichever you pick.

    Your pictures are read one at a time while the GIF is written, so a long list of large photos does not have to fit in memory together. The preview above the button plays the same frames at a smaller size so you can judge the order, the speed and the fit before you commit; it pauses with one button, and starts paused if your system asks for reduced motion.

    Tips

    • Keep it short: a few seconds at 10 frames a second is plenty, and fewer, smaller frames give a much smaller file.
    • Make the pictures the same shape first with Crop image, or shrink big photos with Resize image.
    • Want one picture instead of an animation? Merge images joins them side by side or in a grid.
