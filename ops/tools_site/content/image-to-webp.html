    Why WebP files are smaller

    WebP is an image format made for the web. It can store a picture lossily, like JPEG, by dropping detail the eye is least likely to miss, or losslessly, like PNG, keeping every pixel. It also supports transparency and, in the lossy form, does so without the large size of a PNG. At the same visible quality a WebP photo is usually 25 to 35 percent smaller than a JPEG, which is why many sites send WebP to browsers that can read it.

    The quality slider sets how much detail the lossy encoder may throw away. A high value keeps fine texture and costs more bytes; a low one gives small files but shows blockiness and smearing around edges and in smooth skies. Numbers around 75 to 85 are where most pictures look unchanged at normal size and the file is much lighter. Screenshots and flat graphics are the other way round: they have large uniform areas that compress very well even at high quality.

    Your browser does the work. Each image is decoded, drawn onto a canvas and saved with the browser's own WebP encoder, then the new size is set beside the old one. Nothing is uploaded. If you convert a single image the page also shows the middle of the original and of the WebP side by side at full size, which is the quickest way to judge whether the quality is high enough.

    Tips

    • Large photos shrink most when you also reduce their dimensions: use Resize image first, then convert.
    • Need a file that old software can open? WebP to PNG goes back, and PNG to JPG makes the most widely supported photo format.
    • To shrink a PNG or JPG without changing its format, use Compress image.
