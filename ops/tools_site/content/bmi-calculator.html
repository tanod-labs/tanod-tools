What BMI measures, and what it leaves out

Body mass index is a ratio: weight divided by the square of height. It was devised in the 19th century to describe populations, and it has stayed popular because it needs only a scale and a tape measure. The square matters. Doubling height does not double the healthy weight; it roughly quadruples it, which is why tall and short people can be compared on the same scale.

The scale has limits you should keep in mind. Muscle is denser than fat, so someone who trains heavily can land in the overweight band with a low body-fat percentage. The reverse also happens: a person with little muscle can sit in the healthy band while carrying more fat than is ideal. BMI also says nothing about age-related changes, ethnic differences in body composition, or where weight is distributed. Doctors usually combine it with other measurements.

When you switch units, the numbers you typed are converted and rounded to one decimal, and the BMI itself is always computed from kilograms and metres. As a check, 5 ft 9 in and 160 lb gives 23.6, and 70 kg at 175 cm gives 22.9. The healthy range at the top of the result is simply the BMI band 18.5 to 24.9 turned back into weights for the height you entered.
