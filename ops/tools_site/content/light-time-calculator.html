The formula

The delay is t = d / c, where d is the distance and c = 299,792.458 km/s is the speed of light in vacuum, exact by the 1983 definition of the metre (CGPM). A round trip is 2d / c. An astronomical unit is exactly 149,597,870.7 km (IAU 2012 Resolution B2).

The Moon's distances are the typical perigee, mean and apogee values of 363,300 km, 384,400 km and 405,500 km from the NASA Moon Fact Sheet, and the Sun's are the perihelion, mean and aphelion distances of about 147.1, 149.6 and 152.1 million km. For the planets, the semi-major axes in astronomical units come from the NASA planetary fact sheets (Mercury 0.387, Venus 0.723, Mars 1.524, Jupiter 5.203, Saturn 9.537, Uranus 19.19, Neptune 30.07, Pluto 39.48). The closest and farthest distances from Earth are estimated as |a − 1| and a + 1 astronomical units, assuming circular orbits in one plane, and the typical value is their midpoint.

These are averages for a quick idea. Real distances depend on the date, the eccentricity and the inclination of the orbits, and radio links also add processing and relay delays. Not for navigation or mission planning.
