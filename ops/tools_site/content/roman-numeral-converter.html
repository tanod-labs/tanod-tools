    How Roman numerals work

    Roman numerals are an additive system with one twist. Symbols are written from largest to smallest and their values are added: XVI is 10 + 5 + 1 = 16. The twist is the subtractive pair: a smaller symbol placed immediately before a larger one is taken away from it, so IX is 10 − 1 = 9. Only six pairs are used (IV, IX, XL, XC, CD and CM), and that restriction is what makes the standard form unique, with exactly one numeral per number.

    A reliable way to write any number is to take it one decimal place at a time. 1994 is one thousand (M), nine hundred (CM), ninety (XC) and four (IV), which is MCMXCIV. Reading is the reverse: scan left to right, add each symbol, and subtract it instead when the next symbol is larger. The breakdown under the converter shows each of these pieces so you can follow the arithmetic.

    Because the largest everyday symbol is M, the strict system tops out at 3999 (MMMCMXCIX); a fourth M would break the three-in-a-row limit. Larger values were written in various ways, including a bar over a symbol to multiply it by a thousand. That bar, the vinculum, is a later convention and not used consistently, which is why the extended range here is optional. Roman numerals are still found on clock faces, building cornerstones, film copyright dates, book prefaces and the numbering of sequels and sporting events.
