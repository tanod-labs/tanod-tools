Calendar arithmetic without surprises

Adding days is straightforward, because a day is always one step on the calendar. Adding months and years is where calendars bite: months have 28 to 31 days, so one month after the 31st may not exist. Software has to choose, and the usual choice, used here, is to clamp to the last day of the target month. That is why 31 January plus one month is 28 February (29 in a leap year), and why adding a month and then taking one away from a month-end date may not bring you back to where you began.

Business days are a different count again. Skipping weekends turns 10 business days into exactly two calendar weeks when you start on a weekday, but the count shifts when you start on a Saturday or Sunday: the first business day after Saturday is Monday. Because holidays vary by country, state and employer, they are left out; if your deadline excludes them, add one business day for each holiday that falls in the range.

The extra facts help when planning. The ISO week number is common in scheduling, payroll and logistics in many countries, and a year can have 52 or 53 of them. The day of the year is useful for ordinal dates, such as 2024-366 for 31 December in a leap year. All of this uses plain calendar arithmetic in UTC, so a change of clocks in your local zone cannot shift the answer by a day.
