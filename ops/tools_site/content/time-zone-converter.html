    Why converting times across zones goes wrong

    A time zone is not just an offset from UTC. It is a set of rules, kept in the IANA time-zone database, that says what the offset was and will be on each date. That is why the same city is UTC-5 in winter and UTC-4 in summer, why two cities that share an offset today may part ways next month, and why a meeting set for 9:00 in London moves relative to New York for a few weeks each spring and autumn, when the two countries change their clocks on different dates.

    Daylight saving transitions create two awkward cases. In spring a stretch of local time is skipped, so a time inside it does not exist. In autumn a stretch repeats, so a time inside it is ambiguous and needs the offset to settle which occurrence is meant. Many programs quietly pick one answer; this converter tells you about the gap or the overlap and, for the overlap, lets you pick.

    For scheduling, comparing clock readings is only half the job. Fairness depends on whose working hours the meeting falls in. The hourly planner lines up every place on the same instants so you can see at a glance where 09:00 to 17:00 overlaps, and where someone would be joining from the middle of the night. Even with a very good overlap, state the zone explicitly in the invitation, preferably with the UTC offset, so nobody has to guess.
